What is epidemiology?

A Broad definition

Epidemiology is the study of how diseases and health-related events are distributed in populations and the factors that influence or determine this distribution1

  • Modern epidemiology often described as starting with John Snow’s studies of a cholera epidemic in London in 1854
Type Description
Descriptive Focuses on describing the frequency, pattern, and distribution of diseases in a population
Analytic Aims to identify the factors that contribute to disease occurrence
Experimental Involves actively manipulating exposure factors to study their effects

Briefly, what is epidemiology? Broadly, it’s the study of the distribution, patterns, and causes of disease in populations. Here is a definition from a famous epidemiology text book.

Does anyone know what the map on the left is?

It’s a map of cholera cases in 1854 London. There was a severe cholera outbreak where 616 people died. Interestingly, most of the patients were taken to a hospital where their treatment was superintended by Florence Nightingale, an incredibly important figure in nursing, social reform, and statistics and data visualization. But it was John Snow who studied the outbreak and hypothesized that germ-contaminated water - not something in the air called “miasma” - was the cause.

On the map, he plotted locations where individuals contracted cholera as well as the location of two water pumps. It showed that cholera cases clustered in homes served by the Southwark and Vauxhall company but not the Lambeth Waterworks pump. And he statistically showed that houses served by the Southwark pump had a cholera mortality rate 14 times that than houses served by Lambeth Waterworks.

The Broad Street cholera outbreak is regarded as the founding event of epidemiology.

There are different types of epidemiology, often broken up into descriptive, analytic, and experimental.

  1. Gordis L. Epidemiology. 5. ed. Elsevier Saunders; 2014.

Epidemiologic studies

  • Designed and thoughtfully implemented to reduce bias and represent underlying sampled populations
    • Interview people via phone, internet, or in-person visits
    • Gather biological measures
    • Make observations about environmental exposures and outcomes

Regardless of the type of epidemiology, epidemiologic studies are known for their design and are thoughfully implemented to reduce bias and represent underlying sampled populations.

Traditionally, data are gathered by interviews or surveys, gather data on exposure information and biological measures, and make observations about environmental exposures and outcomes.

Today I’ll talk about some data sources that are often used in epidemiology.

U.S Decennial Census

  • Mandated every 10 years by the U.S. Constitution, started 1790
  • Goal is to have proper representation and taxation in U.S.
  • Conducted by the U.S. Census Bureau
  • Data is widely used by scientists for research
  • Geographic and sociodemographic variables also lay foundation for epidemiologic research
  • Full data is released 72 years after census takes place
  • U.S. population according the 2020 census1: 331,449,281

One that you might be familiar with is the decennial census. It is famously mandated to be collected every 10 years by the US constitution primarily for allocating representation in government and taxes. The data is widely used by scientists for research because, as a census, attempts to directly collect information on all individuals in the population, with its geographic and sociodemographic variables serving as the gold standard for population estimates.

According to the 2020 Census, the US population was 331,449,281

  1. https://www.census.gov

Information that is part of the census

  • Geography
  • Population
    • Sex, age, race, ethnicity, household relationship, type and size, family type and size, and group quarters
  • Housing
    • Occupancy status, vacancy status, and tenure

Source: Standard Hierarchy of Census Geographic Entities

The Census collects primarily geographic and sociodemographic variables. The geographic information is abstracted based on the address associated with the Census form and provides various localities like state, county, ZIP code, census tract and census block.

It additionally collects a person’s sex, age and birth date, and race/ethnicity status, along with information about the household - how many people live there. This information is relevant for health researchers because it can give a sense of how representative a study sample is of the US population and these data can be used to employ statistical techniques to make results from a study sample more representative of the US population.

U.S. Geographic Codes

  • Federal Information Processing Standard (FIPS) codes
    • Maintained by the American National Standards Institute (ANSI)
    • Uniquely identifies counties and county equivalents
  • States
    • Postal code: PA
    • FIPS code: 42
  • County
    • Name: Philadelphia County
    • County FIPS code: 101
    • Full FIPS code: 42101
  • ZIP codes / ZIP Code Tabulation Areas
  • Census tracts
  • Census blocks

Zone Improvement Plan or ZIP codes are a postal code system for mail delivery. There are some interesting considerations regarding use of ZIP codes, primarily that they can cross county lines. Additionally, ZIP codes refer to addresses along a network of roads. The US Census Bureau has created ZIP Code Tabulation Areas that define the land covered by/associated with each ZIP code.

Census tracts are smaller units divided up to contain approximately 4,000 individuals each and are supposed to represent a relatively homogenous population. Unlike ZIP Codes, these boundaries respect county and state lines.

Census blocks are even smaller. There were over 8,100,000 census blocks in the US and Puerto Rico for the 2020 Census

American Community Survey (ACS)

  • The US Census Bureau gathers data continuously to estimate regional socioeconomic and housing characteristics
  • Data is released in aggregated 1-, 3-, and 5-year products
  • Information collected helps determine how more than $675 billion in federal and state funds are distributed each year

As early as James Madison in 1790 envisioned collecting additional, long form data on the US population.

In the 1940s, a long-form version of the Census was sent out to a subset of households.

After the 1960 Census, officials and some in the private sector wanted more timely long-form-type data.

In 1981 a rolling sample design was proposed and was adopted in 2005 so the ACS could continuously collect data. Data are now released annually.

It collects additional information beyong the Census including more details about housing and income, educational attainment, ability, and occupation.

Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS)

  • Ongoing CDC-led phone survey to collect risk behavior assoicated with premature morbidity and mortality among US adults
    • Started in 1984 with 15 states
    • Nationwide in 1993
    • Cellphone interviews included starting in 2011
  • State health departments collect state information and send to CDC
    • Includes 50 states, DC, Puerto Rico, US Virgin Islands, Guam, American Samoa, Palau
  • Completes ~400,000 interviews each year

As of 2023, 99.2% of occupied households had telephone service.

What has BRFSS been used for?

  • Provides information to establish policies and strategies to promote health
    • Alcohol, smoking, sugary drink consumption, seatbelt use
  • Vaccine availability/compliance
    • Influenza vaccine shortage 2004-2005
  • Response to urgent issues:
    • During 2009 H1N1 flu pandemic: influenza-like illness module H1N1 vaccination questions added
    • Following Hurricane Katrina and Rita in 2005: monitor health outcomes

Binge drinking among adults 2011

Defined as 5 or more drinks on an occasion for men, 4 for women

BRFSS sampling

  • Attempts to conduct 4,000 interviews per state per year
  • Landlines (80%)
    • Numbers drawn from two strata based on presumed density of household numbers
    • Randomly sample one eligible adult per household
  • Cell phones (20%)
    • Numbers drawn by area code (if people moved, complete interview, then give results to appropriate state)
    • Treated as single adult households

BRFSS questionnaire

  • Standard core: included each year, asked in all states

  • Rotating core: asked by all states every other year

  • Optional modules: states select which to include

  • State-added questions: state-specific questions that are cognitively tested

  • Protocols to conduct survey set by CDC:

    • 20% interviews on weekdays
    • 80% interviews on week nights and weekends
    • Up to 15 attempts for each phone number
    • Survey “complete” if data collected for age, race, sex

Optional modules for 2024 included prediabetes and diabetes, arthritis, shingles and HPV vaccination, cognitive decline, caregiver health, cancer survivorship (type, treatment, pain management), adverse childhood experiences, sugar-sweetened beverages, and tobacco cessation.

BRFSS weighting

  • Data weighting is performed to reduce bias
  • Design weighting accounts for
    • Number of phones and adults in household
    • Number of records selected within geographic and density strata
  • Raking weights: since 2011, ensures data represents population by
    • Sex, age, race, education, marital status, home ownership, phone ownership, sub-state region
  • Final weight for each respondent =
    • design weight * raking weight
The survey R package in R can be used to analyze BRFSS data accounting for weights

Analyzing BRFSS data

  • All BRFSS data should be analyzed using complex sampling procedures
  • Lack of attention to the complex survey design specifications in analyses will yield biased estimates of population prevalence and of associated confidence intervals
  • Useful documentation:
    • BRFSS documentation

Youth Risk Factor Surveillance System (YRBSS)

  • CDC-led survey to monitor priority health-risk behaviors: those that are preventable and contribute to morbidity and mortality among youth and young adults
    • Conducts school-based national, state, tribal, and large urban school district surveys of representative samples of high school (and some middle school) students, along with national surveys and special population surveys
    • Data is representative of 9-12 graders in the US
    • Topics: injury/violence, sexual behaviors, tobacco use, alcohol and drug use, unhealthy diet, physical activity, obesity, asthma
  • Completes ~10,000-15,000 interviews each year

National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)

  • CDC study to assess health and nutritional status of adults and children in the US
    • Started in 1960
    • Since 1999, continuously examines 5,000 persons/year in 2-year waves
    • 15 counties remain same, others change each year
  • Sets national standards (e.g. height, weight, blood pressure)
  • Questionnaires completed via in-person home interviews and measures taken during physical exam
  • Some exposures measured via additional monitors
  • Oversamples groups of particular public health interest - racial/ethnic minorities, youth, and elderly - to ensure sufficient data in these subgroups

NHANES Sampling Procedure

After data collection, weights are modified for non-response and then trimmed (reduce impact of any extreme weight values) and calibrated (ensuring weighted estimates align with population totals, accounting for over/undersampling)

Biases in EHR data

There are sources of statistical bias – systematic and random error.

Systematic bias: consistent error associated with a flawed study design Random error: this is variability that arises from random fluctuations

Random error can lead to wrong conclusions, though this can be mitigated by increasing sample size. Systematic errors, like selection bias and missing data, do not go away as sample size increases. In fact, these biases can be amplified and our false confidence in our results may increase with N - Increasing dataset size does not always lead to better statistical inference - Poor quality or unrepresentative data can amplify biases - “our false confidence may increase with \(n\)”

Why weighting matters

  • Most clinical cohorts, EHR-linked biobanks are convenience samples from healthcare systems, making them inherently non-representative
  • Hospital-based studies often oversample sicker, older, or higher-utilizing populations
  • Geographic clustering means local cohorts may not reflect national diversity
  • Systematic sources of bias do not go away as sample size increases; large \(N\) can amplify these biases!
  • Post-hoc weights can help adjust for these selection biases and improve generalizability
  • Resources like Census/ACS, BRFSS, and NHANES provide information that can be used to generate weights to make study samples more representative of a desired target population (e.g., a metro area, a state, the country)
